Intext Questions

Q1: If a trait A exists in 10% of a population of an asexually reproducing species and a trait B exists in 60% of the same population, which trait is likely to have arisen earlier?
Answer: Trait B is likely to have arisen earlier. In an asexually reproducing species, variations occur naturally over time and are passed down to offspring. Since trait B is present in a larger percentage of the population (60%), it means it has been accumulating and passing down for a much longer period compared to trait A (10%).
Q2: How does the creation of variations in a species promote survival?
Answer: Variations help a species adapt to drastic changes in their environment, such as changes in temperature, water levels, or the introduction of new predators. Individuals with favorable variations are more likely to survive these harsh changes and reproduce, ensuring the survival of the species as a whole.
Q3: How do Mendel's experiments show that traits may be dominant or recessive?
Answer: Mendel crossed pure tall pea plants (TT) with pure short pea plants (tt). In the first generation (F1), all the plants produced were tall (Tt), showing that the tall trait is dominant and the short trait is recessive (it remained hidden). However, when the F1 plants were self-pollinated, the F2 generation produced tall and short plants in a 3:1 ratio, proving the recessive trait was still present and could be expressed in subsequent generations.
Q4: How do Mendel's experiments show that traits are inherited independently?
Answer: Mendel performed a dihybrid cross by taking two pairs of contrasting characteristics, such as seed shape (Round/Wrinkled) and seed color (Yellow/Green). When he crossed plants with round-yellow seeds and wrinkled-green seeds, the F2 generation produced new combinations like round-green and wrinkled-yellow seeds. This proved that the traits for seed shape and seed color separate and are inherited independently of each other.
