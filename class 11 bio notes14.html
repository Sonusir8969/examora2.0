NCERT Solutions for Class 11 Biology Chapter 14 Breathing and Exchange of Gases

NCERT Solutions for Class 11 Biology Chapter 14 Breathing and Exchange of Gases covers the human respiratory system, the mechanism of breathing, exchange and transport of gases, and regulation of respiration.

1. Respiratory Organs and Mechanism of Breathing

Short Note:

• The human respiratory system includes the nostrils, nasal passage, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, and alveoli within the lungs.

• Breathing (pulmonary ventilation) involves inspiration (diaphragm contracts and moves down, ribs move up and out, increasing thoracic volume, decreasing pressure, air rushes in) and expiration (reverse process).

2. Exchange of Gases

Short Note:

• Exchange of O2 and CO2 occurs by simple diffusion, mainly determined by pressure/concentration gradients, solubility of gases, and the thickness of the alveolar membrane, occurring at the alveoli (with blood) and at tissues (with blood).

3. Transport of Gases

Short Note:

• About 97% of O2 is transported by haemoglobin as oxyhaemoglobin; the rest (~3%) is dissolved in plasma.

• CO2 is transported mainly (~70%) as bicarbonate ions (HCO3−), about 20-25% bound to haemoglobin as carbamino-haemoglobin, and about 7% dissolved in plasma.

4. Regulation of Respiration

Short Note:

• Respiration is regulated by a respiratory rhythm centre in the medulla of the brain, and a pneumotaxic centre in the pons, which fine-tunes the rate; chemosensitive areas respond to CO2 and H+ concentration to adjust breathing rate.

NCERT Textbook Exercise Questions

Define vital capacity. What is its significance?
Answer: Vital capacity is the maximum volume of air a person can breathe out after a forced (maximum) inspiration, equal to the sum of tidal volume, inspiratory reserve volume, and expiratory reserve volume. It is significant as it indicates the maximum amount of air a person can move into or out of their lungs, useful for assessing lung function/health.
What is the effect of pCO2 on the oxygen-binding capacity of haemoglobin?
Answer: A higher pCO2 (along with lower pO2, higher H+ concentration, and higher temperature) decreases the affinity of haemoglobin for oxygen, favouring the dissociation of oxyhaemoglobin (releasing O2) — this is beneficial at the tissue level (where CO2 is high) to facilitate O2 unloading for cellular use.
How is CO2 transported in the blood?
Answer: CO2 is transported in three main forms: about 7% dissolved directly in plasma, about 20-25% bound to haemoglobin as carbamino-haemoglobin, and the majority, about 70%, is transported as bicarbonate ions (HCO3−), formed when CO2 combines with water (catalysed by carbonic anhydrase in RBCs) to form carbonic acid, which dissociates into H+ and HCO3−.
Explain the process of inspiration and expiration in humans.
Answer: During inspiration, the diaphragm contracts and flattens, and the external intercostal muscles contract, raising the ribs and sternum, which increases the volume of the thoracic cavity, decreasing pulmonary pressure below atmospheric pressure so that air flows into the lungs. During expiration, these muscles relax, decreasing thoracic volume and increasing pulmonary pressure above atmospheric pressure, causing air to be pushed out of the lungs.
