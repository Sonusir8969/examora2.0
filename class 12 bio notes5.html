NCERT Solutions for Class 12 Biology Chapter 5 Molecular Basis of Inheritance

NCERT Solutions for Class 12 Biology Chapter 5 Molecular Basis of Inheritance covers DNA structure and replication, transcription, the genetic code, translation, and gene regulation via the lac operon.

1. DNA Structure and the Search for Genetic Material

Short Note:

• Experiments by Griffith (transformation), Avery, MacLeod and McCarty, and Hershey-Chase confirmed that DNA, not protein, is the hereditary genetic material.

• DNA is a double helix (Watson-Crick model) made of two anti-parallel polynucleotide strands held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary nitrogenous bases: adenine pairs with thymine (2 hydrogen bonds), and guanine pairs with cytosine (3 hydrogen bonds).

2. DNA Replication

Short Note:

• DNA replication is semi-conservative, meaning each daughter DNA molecule retains one parental strand and synthesises one new strand, as demonstrated by the Meselson and Stahl experiment.

• The enzyme DNA polymerase catalyses the synthesis of new DNA strands using the parental strands as templates, and replication occurs during the S-phase of the cell cycle.

3. Transcription and the Genetic Code

Short Note:

• Transcription is the process of copying genetic information from one strand of DNA into messenger RNA (mRNA), carried out by the enzyme RNA polymerase, and in eukaryotes, the primary transcript undergoes processing (splicing, capping, tailing) to form mature mRNA.

• The genetic code is a triplet code (a codon of three nucleotides specifies one amino acid), is nearly universal across organisms, is degenerate (most amino acids are coded by more than one codon), and includes start (AUG) and stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA).

4. Translation and Gene Regulation (Lac Operon)

Short Note:

• Translation is the process of polypeptide (protein) synthesis using the sequence of codons on mRNA as a template, occurring on ribosomes with the help of tRNA molecules that bring specific amino acids.

• The lac operon in E. coli is a classic example of gene regulation, where the structural genes for lactose metabolism are switched on in the presence of lactose (the inducer) and switched off in its absence, illustrating how gene expression can be regulated at the transcriptional level.

NCERT Textbook Exercise Questions

Q1: Why did Hershey and Chase's experiment prove that DNA, not protein, is the genetic material?
Answer: Hershey and Chase used bacteriophages labelled separately with radioactive sulphur (in protein) and radioactive phosphorus (in DNA) to infect bacteria. They found that only the radioactive phosphorus (DNA) entered the bacterial cells and was involved in producing new phage particles, while the radioactive protein remained outside, proving conclusively that DNA, and not protein, is the genetic material that is passed on during infection and replication.
Q2: Explain the semi-conservative mode of DNA replication with reference to the Meselson-Stahl experiment.
Answer: In semi-conservative replication, each new DNA molecule consists of one original (parental) strand and one newly synthesised strand. Meselson and Stahl grew E. coli in a medium with heavy nitrogen (15N) and then transferred it to normal nitrogen (14N) medium, tracking the density of DNA over generations using centrifugation. Their results showed a hybrid (intermediate density) DNA after one generation and a mix of hybrid and light DNA after two generations, confirming that replication is semi-conservative rather than conservative or dispersive.
Q3: What are the salient features of the genetic code?
Answer: The genetic code is a triplet code, where a sequence of three nucleotides (codon) specifies one amino acid; it is nearly universal, meaning the same codon specifies the same amino acid in almost all organisms; it is degenerate, since most amino acids are coded for by more than one codon; it is unambiguous, as each codon codes for only one specific amino acid; and it includes one start codon (AUG) and three stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA) that do not code for any amino acid.
Q4: Describe the regulation of the lac operon in the presence and absence of lactose.
Answer: In the absence of lactose, the repressor protein binds to the operator region of the lac operon, blocking RNA polymerase from transcribing the structural genes, so the operon remains switched off. In the presence of lactose, a metabolite of lactose (allolactose) acts as an inducer, binding to the repressor and changing its shape so it can no longer bind to the operator; this allows RNA polymerase to transcribe the structural genes, producing enzymes needed to metabolise lactose, thus switching the operon on.
