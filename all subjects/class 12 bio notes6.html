NCERT Solutions for Class 12 Biology Chapter 6 Evolution

NCERT Solutions for Class 12 Biology Chapter 6 Evolution covers the origin of life, evidence for evolution, Darwin's theory of natural selection, Hardy-Weinberg principle, and human evolution.

1. Origin of Life and Evidence for Evolution

Short Note:

• The chemical evolution theory, supported by the Miller-Urey experiment, suggests that simple organic molecules could have formed spontaneously from inorganic gases under early Earth conditions, eventually giving rise to the first life forms.

• Evidence for evolution includes fossil records (showing gradual change over geological time), comparative anatomy (homologous organs indicating common ancestry, and analogous organs indicating convergent evolution), and biogeography (distribution patterns of species across continents).

2. Darwin's Theory of Natural Selection

Short Note:

• Charles Darwin proposed that natural selection acts on heritable variations within a population; individuals better adapted to their environment survive and reproduce more successfully, passing on favourable traits to the next generation.

• Classic examples include industrial melanism in peppered moths (where darker moths became more common in polluted areas due to better camouflage) and the development of antibiotic resistance in bacteria.

3. Hardy-Weinberg Principle and Mechanisms of Evolution

Short Note:

• The Hardy-Weinberg principle states that allele and genotype frequencies in a population remain constant across generations in the absence of evolutionary influences, serving as a null model to detect whether evolution is occurring.

• Factors that disturb this genetic equilibrium and drive evolution include mutation, gene migration/flow, genetic drift (random changes in allele frequency, especially significant in small populations), and natural selection.

4. Human Evolution

Short Note:

• Human evolutionary studies, based on fossil evidence, suggest that early hominids originated in Africa, evolving through stages such as Australopithecus, Homo habilis, Homo erectus, and Homo neanderthalensis, eventually leading to modern Homo sapiens.

• Key evolutionary trends observed include increasing brain capacity, development of bipedalism (upright walking), and tool use, which distinguished later hominids from earlier ancestors.

NCERT Textbook Exercise Questions

Q1: What was the significance of the Miller-Urey experiment?
Answer: The Miller-Urey experiment simulated the early Earth's atmosphere (containing methane, ammonia, hydrogen and water vapour) and passed electric sparks through this mixture to mimic lightning. The experiment resulted in the formation of simple amino acids, providing experimental support for the idea that complex organic molecules, the building blocks of life, could have formed spontaneously from inorganic substances under early Earth conditions.
Q2: Explain the concept of natural selection with a suitable example.
Answer: Natural selection is the process by which individuals with heritable traits better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more successfully than others, gradually increasing the frequency of favourable traits in a population. A classic example is industrial melanism in the peppered moth, where dark-coloured moths, previously rare, became more common in polluted, soot-covered areas of England because their dark colour provided better camouflage against predators compared to the lighter-coloured moths.
Q3: State the Hardy-Weinberg principle and explain its significance.
Answer: The Hardy-Weinberg principle states that allele frequencies and genotype frequencies in a sufficiently large, randomly mating population remain constant from generation to generation in the absence of factors like mutation, migration, genetic drift, and selection. Its significance lies in providing a mathematical baseline (p² + 2pq + q² = 1) against which real populations can be compared to detect whether evolutionary changes are occurring.
Q4: What are the main stages in human evolution?
Answer: Human evolution is believed to have progressed through several stages including Dryopithecus and Ramapithecus (early ape-like ancestors), Australopithecus (early bipedal hominids), Homo habilis (early tool users), Homo erectus (with larger brain size and advanced tool use), Homo neanderthalensis, and finally Homo sapiens (modern humans), with fossil evidence suggesting an African origin for the human lineage.
