NCERT Solutions for Class 11 Biology Chapter 15 Body Fluids and Circulation

NCERT Solutions for Class 11 Biology Chapter 15 Body Fluids and Circulation covers revision notes and step-by-step textbook solutions detailing blood composition, blood groups, blood clotting, lymph, human heart structure, cardiac cycle, electrocardiogram (ECG), double circulation, regulation of cardiac activity, and circulatory disorders.

1. Blood Composition, Groups & Coagulation

Short Note:

• Plasma (~55% of blood): Straw-colored fluid containing 90–92% water and proteins (Fibrinogen for clotting, Globulins for defense, Albumins for osmotic balance).

• Formed Elements (~45% of blood):

  - Erythrocytes (RBCs): Biconcave, enucleated in mammals, carry hemoglobin (~5–5.5 million/mm³; lifespan ~120 days).

  - Leucocytes (WBCs): Nucleated, defense cells (~6000–8000/mm³). Divided into Granulocytes (Neutrophils, Eosinophils, Basophils) and Agranulocytes (Lymphocytes, Monocytes).

  - Thrombocytes (Platelets): Cell fragments from megakaryocytes (~1,500,000–3,500,000/mm³), essential for blood clotting.

• Blood Groups:

  - ABO System: Based on surface antigens A and B. Type O is universal donor; Type AB is universal recipient.

  - Rh Grouping: Rh positive (+ve) or Rh negative (-ve). Rh incompatibility during pregnancy (Rh⁻ mother with Rh⁺ fetus) can cause Erythroblastosis Fetalis.

• Coagulation of Blood: Injury activates platelets → release Thromboplastin → forms Thrombokinase enzyme → converts inactive Prothrombin to Thrombin (in presence of Ca²⁺) → converts soluble Fibrinogen to insoluble Fibrin threads to form clot.

2. Lymph (Tissue Fluid) & Human Circulatory Pathways

Short Note:

• Lymph (Interstitial Fluid): Colorless fluid containing specialized lymphocytes, lacking RBCs and larger proteins. It transports nutrients, hormones, and absorbs fats via lacteals in intestinal villi.

• Circulatory Patterns:

  - Open Circulatory System: Blood pumped by heart passes through large vessels into open spaces or body cavities called sinuses (e.g., Arthropods and Molluscs).

  - Closed Circulatory System: Blood is circulated through a closed network of blood vessels (e.g., Annelids and Chordates). Provides more precise blood flow regulation.

3. Human Heart, Cardiac Cycle & Electrocardiogram (ECG)

Short Note:

• Structure of Heart: Four-chambered muscular organ (2 atria, 2 ventricles) enclosed in pericardium. Nodal tissue includes SAN (Sino-atrial node) located in right atrium (pacemaker, generates 70–75 action potentials/min) and AVN (Atrio-ventricular node).

• Cardiac Cycle (Duration ~0.8 sec):

  - Joint Diastole: All four chambers relaxed, blood flows from pulmonary veins/vena cava into ventricles through open AV valves (tricuspid/bicuspid).

  - Atrial Systole: SAN generates action potential; both atria contract, increasing blood flow into ventricles by ~30%.

  - Ventricular Systole: Ventricles contract; AV valves close (producing 1st heart sound 'LUB'); semilunar valves open, pumping blood into aorta and pulmonary artery.

  - Ventricular Diastole: Ventricles relax; semilunar valves close (producing 2nd heart sound 'DUB').

• Stroke Volume & Cardiac Output: Stroke volume = ~70 mL per beat. Cardiac Output = Stroke Volume × Heart Rate = 70 mL × 72 = ~5000 mL (5 Litres/min).

• Electrocardiogram (ECG): Graphical representation of electrical activity of heart during cardiac cycle.

  - P-wave: Depolarisation (excitation) of atria.

  - QRS complex: Depolarisation of ventricles (triggers ventricular contraction).

  - T-wave: Repolarisation (return to resting state) of ventricles.

4. Double Circulation & Circulatory System Disorders

Short Note:

• Double Circulation:

  - Pulmonary Circulation: Deoxygenated blood from right ventricle → Pulmonary Artery → Lungs → Oxygenated blood → Pulmonary Veins → Left Atrium.

  - Systemic Circulation: Oxygenated blood from left ventricle → Aorta → Body Tissues → Deoxygenated blood → Vena Cava → Right Atrium.

  - Hepatic Portal System: Vascular connection between digestive tract and liver before blood enters systemic circulation.

• Disorders of Circulatory System:

  - Hypertension (High Blood Pressure): Blood pressure higher than normal (120/80 mmHg). Sustained 140/90 mmHg or higher affects heart, brain, and kidneys.

  - Coronary Artery Disease (CAD / Atherosclerosis): Deposition of calcium, fat, cholesterol, and fibrous tissue in arteries supplying heart muscle, narrowing their lumen.

  - Angina Pectoris: Acute chest pain due to inadequate oxygen reaching heart muscle.

  - Heart Failure: State of heart when it does not pump blood effectively enough to meet the body's needs (congestion of lungs is a main symptom).

Intext & Conceptual Questions

Q1: What is Erythroblastosis Fetalis? How can it be prevented?
Answer:
• Definition: Erythroblastosis Fetalis is a severe hemolytic disease of the newborn caused by Rh incompatibility between an Rh-negative mother and an Rh-positive fetus.
• Mechanism: During the delivery of the first child, small amounts of Rh⁺ fetal blood may mix with maternal blood, causing the mother to synthesize anti-Rh antibodies. During subsequent Rh⁺ pregnancies, these maternal IgG antibodies cross the placenta into fetal blood, destroying fetal RBCs and leading to severe anemia, jaundice, or fetal death.
• Prevention: Administering anti-Rh antibodies (RhoGAM) to the Rh-negative mother immediately following the delivery of the first Rh-positive child.
Q2: What is the significance of the Sino-atrial Node (SAN) being called the pacemaker of the heart?
Answer:
The Sino-atrial Node (SAN) is called the pacemaker of the human heart because:
1. It is a specialized nodal tissue that can generate action potentials automatically without external neural stimulation (auto-excitable).
2. It generates the maximum number of action potentials per minute (~70–75 impulses/min).
3. It initiates and maintains the rhythmic contractile activity (pacing) of the entire heart.

Textbook Chapter End Exercise Questions

Q1: Distinguish between Open and Closed Circulatory Systems.
Answer:
Feature Open Circulatory System Closed Circulatory System
Blood Vessels Blood vessels open into large open spaces called sinuses. Blood flows through a closed network of blood vessels (arteries, veins, capillaries).
Direct Tissue Contact Body tissues remain in direct contact with circulating fluid (hemolymph). Blood does not come into direct contact with tissues; exchange occurs via tissue fluid.
Regulation & Pressure Blood flows at low pressure and cannot be regulated precisely. Blood flows at high pressure, allowing precise regulation of flow rate.
Examples Arthropods, Molluscs. Annelids, Cephalopods, Vertebrates (Humans).
Q2: Distinguish between Arteries and Veins.
Answer:
Feature Arteries Veins
Direction of Flow Carry blood away from heart to various organs. Carry blood from various organs back toward the heart.
Nature of Blood Carry oxygenated blood (except Pulmonary Artery). Carry deoxygenated blood (except Pulmonary Vein).
Wall & Lumen Structure Thick, elastic muscular walls with a narrow lumen. Thinner, less muscular walls with a wide lumen.
Internal Valves Valves are absent (high pressure prevents backflow). Valves are present to prevent backflow of blood under low pressure.
