NCERT Solutions for Class 12 Biology Chapter 4 Principles of Inheritance and Variation

NCERT Solutions for Class 12 Biology Chapter 4 Principles of Inheritance and Variation covers Mendel's laws, deviations from Mendelism, chromosomal theory of inheritance, sex determination, and genetic disorders.

1. Mendel's Laws of Inheritance

Short Note:

• Gregor Mendel, through his experiments on garden pea plants, formulated the Law of Dominance (one allele masks the expression of the other in a heterozygote), the Law of Segregation (the two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete carries only one allele), and the Law of Independent Assortment (alleles of different genes assort independently of one another during gamete formation).

• A monohybrid cross studies the inheritance of a single trait, typically giving a 3:1 phenotypic ratio in the F2 generation, while a dihybrid cross studies two traits together, giving a 9:3:3:1 ratio in F2.

2. Deviations from Mendelian Inheritance

Short Note:

• Incomplete dominance occurs when the heterozygote shows an intermediate phenotype between the two parents (e.g. flower colour in Mirabilis jalapa), while co-dominance occurs when both alleles express themselves fully and simultaneously (e.g. AB blood group in the ABO system).

• Multiple alleles, such as the three alleles (IA, IB, i) controlling human ABO blood groups, and pleiotropy, where a single gene affects multiple phenotypic traits (e.g. phenylketonuria), are further examples of complexity beyond simple Mendelian ratios.

3. Chromosomal Theory and Sex Determination

Short Note:

• The chromosomal theory of inheritance, proposed by Sutton and Boveri, states that genes are located on chromosomes, and their behaviour during meiosis parallels the segregation and assortment of Mendelian factors.

• In humans, sex is determined by sex chromosomes: females have XX and males have XY chromosomes; since sperm can carry either an X or a Y chromosome, the father's gamete determines the sex of the child.

4. Mutation and Genetic Disorders

Short Note:

• Mutations are sudden, heritable changes in DNA sequence; point mutations, such as in sickle cell anaemia, result from a single base pair change leading to an altered protein.

• Genetic disorders can be Mendelian (caused by a single gene mutation, e.g. haemophilia, sickle cell anaemia, colour blindness) or chromosomal (caused by changes in chromosome number or structure, e.g. Down's syndrome due to trisomy of chromosome 21, and Turner's and Klinefelter's syndromes involving sex chromosome abnormalities).

NCERT Textbook Exercise Questions

Q1: State and explain Mendel's Law of Dominance with an example.
Answer: The Law of Dominance states that in a heterozygous condition, one allele (the dominant allele) masks the expression of the other allele (the recessive allele). For example, when Mendel crossed a pure tall pea plant (TT) with a pure short pea plant (tt), all F1 offspring (Tt) were tall, showing that the tall trait was dominant over the short trait, even though the recessive allele for shortness was still present but unexpressed.
Q2: What is the difference between incomplete dominance and co-dominance?
Answer: In incomplete dominance, neither allele is completely dominant, so the heterozygote shows a blended, intermediate phenotype, as seen in the pink flowers of Mirabilis jalapa when red and white flowered plants are crossed. In co-dominance, both alleles are expressed fully and independently in the heterozygote without blending, as seen in the AB blood group, where both A and B antigens are simultaneously expressed on the red blood cells.
Q3: Explain the chromosomal basis of sex determination in humans.
Answer: In humans, females possess two X chromosomes (XX) while males possess one X and one Y chromosome (XY). During gamete formation, all eggs carry a single X chromosome, but sperm may carry either an X or a Y chromosome in equal proportion. If a sperm carrying an X chromosome fertilises the egg, the offspring will be female (XX), and if a sperm carrying a Y chromosome fertilises the egg, the offspring will be male (XY), meaning the father's gamete determines the sex of the child.
Q4: What is Down's syndrome, and what causes it?
Answer: Down's syndrome is a genetic disorder caused by the presence of an extra copy of chromosome 21, resulting in a total of 47 chromosomes instead of the usual 46 (a condition called trisomy 21). This chromosomal abnormality typically arises due to non-disjunction of chromosome 21 during gamete formation, and it leads to characteristic physical features and varying degrees of intellectual disability in affected individuals.
