1. Ecosystem Structure and Productivity

Short Note:

• An ecosystem consists of biotic (living organisms) and abiotic (physical/chemical) components interacting together, and is structured into producers (autotrophs, mainly green plants), consumers (herbivores, carnivores, omnivores) and decomposers (fungi and bacteria that break down organic matter).

• Primary productivity refers to the amount of biomass or organic matter produced by producers per unit area over a period of time, divided into Gross Primary Productivity (GPP, total production) and Net Primary Productivity (NPP, production remaining after respiratory losses).

2. Decomposition

Short Note:

• Decomposition is the process by which decomposers break down complex organic matter (detritus) present in dead plant and animal remains into simpler inorganic substances, occurring through the steps of fragmentation, leaching, catabolism, humification and mineralisation.

• The rate of decomposition is influenced by the chemical composition of detritus and climatic factors such as temperature and soil moisture, with warm, moist conditions generally favouring faster decomposition.

3. Energy Flow and Ecological Pyramids

Short Note:

• Energy flow in an ecosystem is unidirectional, moving from producers to various levels of consumers, and follows the 10 per cent law, meaning only about 10% of energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next, with the rest lost mainly as heat during metabolic processes.

• Ecological pyramids represent the trophic structure of an ecosystem and can depict number, biomass, or energy at each trophic level; the pyramid of energy is always upright, while pyramids of number and biomass can sometimes be inverted (e.g. pyramid of numbers in a tree ecosystem).

4. Nutrient Cycling

Short Note:

• Biogeochemical cycles, such as the carbon cycle and phosphorus cycle, describe the continuous circulation of essential nutrients between living organisms and the abiotic environment.

• The carbon cycle primarily involves the exchange of carbon dioxide between the atmosphere and living organisms through photosynthesis and respiration, while the phosphorus cycle, lacking a significant atmospheric component, mainly involves the weathering of rocks as the primary source of phosphorus.

NCERT Textbook Exercise Questions

Q1: Differentiate between Gross Primary Productivity (GPP) and Net Primary Productivity (NPP).
Answer: Gross Primary Productivity (GPP) is the total rate of production of organic matter (biomass) by producers through photosynthesis in a given area over a specific time period. Net Primary Productivity (NPP) is the biomass or energy that remains after subtracting the amount used by producers for their own respiration (R) from the GPP, expressed as NPP = GPP - R, and this remaining energy is what is available to the consumers in the ecosystem.
Q2: Explain the 10 per cent law of energy transfer in ecosystems.
Answer: The 10 per cent law, proposed by Lindeman, states that only about 10 per cent of the energy available at one trophic level is transferred to the next trophic level in an ecosystem's food chain, with the remaining 90 per cent being lost mainly as heat during respiration and other metabolic processes, or remaining unused/undigested. This significant energy loss at each step is the reason why food chains typically consist of only a limited number of trophic levels, usually not exceeding four or five.
Q3: Why is the pyramid of biomass sometimes inverted, while the pyramid of energy is always upright?
Answer: The pyramid of biomass can be inverted in certain ecosystems, such as a pond ecosystem, where the biomass of the producers (phytoplankton) at any given time may be lower than that of the consumers (zooplankton) that feed on them, due to the rapid turnover rate of the producers. The pyramid of energy, however, is always upright because energy transfer between trophic levels always follows the 10 per cent law, meaning energy available at a higher trophic level can never exceed that of the level below it.
Q4: What are the main steps involved in the process of decomposition?
Answer: Decomposition involves several sequential steps: fragmentation, where detritivores break down detritus into smaller particles; leaching, where water-soluble inorganic nutrients seep into the soil; catabolism, where bacterial and fungal enzymes break down detritus into simpler inorganic substances; humification, leading to the accumulation of a dark, amorphous substance called humus that is resistant to microbial action; and mineralisation, where humus is further degraded, releasing inorganic nutrients back into the soil.
