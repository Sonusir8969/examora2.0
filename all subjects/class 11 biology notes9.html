NCERT Solutions for Class 11 Biology Chapter 9 Biomolecules

NCERT Solutions for Class 11 Biology Chapter 9 Biomolecules covers primary and secondary metabolites, biomacromolecules (proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, nucleic acids), protein structures, bond types, and enzyme kinetics and classification.

1. Analysis of Chemical Composition & Metabolites

Short Note:

• Chemical Analysis: Living tissue ground in Trichloroacetic acid (CCl3COOH) yields two fractions when filtered through cheesecloth:

  1. Acid-soluble Pool (Filtrate): Contains biomicromolecules with molecular weights ranging from 18 to 800 Daltons (e.g., amino acids, monosaccharides, nucleotides).

  2. Acid-insoluble Fraction (Retentate): Contains biomacromolecules with molecular weights above 10,000 Daltons (e.g., proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides).

• Lipids Special Case: Lipids have molecular weights under 800 Da, but form membrane vesicles during grinding that stay in the acid-insoluble fraction. Hence, lipids are macromolecular fractions though strictly not macromolecules.

• Primary Metabolites: Direct involvement in normal physiological functions and growth (e.g., glucose, amino acids, ribose).

• Secondary Metabolites: Produced by plants, fungi, and microbes with ecological importance (e.g., Alkaloids like Morphine, Pigments like Carotenoids, Toxins like Abrin/Ricin, Drugs like Vinblastine/Curcumin, Polymeric substances like Rubber/Gums).

2. Biomacromolecules & Proteins

Short Note:

• Amino Acids: Organic compounds containing an amino group (-NH2) and an acidic carboxyl group (-COOH) attached to the same α-carbon (substituted methanes).

  - Vary based on 'R' group (Glycine - H, Alanine - CH3, Serine - CH2OH).

  - Existing as Zwitterions at specific pH levels with simultaneous positive and negative charges.

• Proteins: Heteropolymers of amino acids linked sequentially by peptide bonds (-CO-NH- formed via dehydration synthesis).

  - Collagen: Most abundant protein in the animal world.

  - RuBisCO: Most abundant protein in the entire biosphere.

3. Polysaccharides, Nucleic Acids & Protein Structure

Short Note:

• Polysaccharides: Long chains of monosaccharides linked by glycosidic bonds.

  - Homopolysaccharides: Single monomer unit (e.g., Starch, Cellulose, Glycogen, Chitin).

  - Starch: Forms helical secondary structures holding iodine molecules, turning blue-black with I2 (cellulose lacks helices and cannot hold I2).

• Nucleic Acids: Polymers of nucleotides linked by phosphodiester bonds.

  - Nucleotide = Nitrogenous Base + Pentose Sugar + Phosphate group.

  - Bases: Purines (Adenine, Guanine) and Pyrimidines (Cytosine, Thymine, Uracil).

• Levels of Protein Structure:

  1. Primary Structure: Positional sequence of amino acids (N-terminal first, C-terminal last).

  2. Secondary Structure: Helical folding (α-helix or β-pleated sheets) stabilized by hydrogen bonds.

  3. Tertiary Structure: 3D folding forming active sites; necessary for biological activity.

  4. Quaternary Structure: Spatial arrangement of multiple polypeptide subunits (e.g., Hemoglobin with 2α and 2β subunits).

4. Enzymes & Nature of Enzyme Action

Short Note:

• Biological catalysts (mostly proteins, except catalytic RNAs called Ribozymes) that lower the activation energy required for a chemical reaction.

• Active Site: Specific pocket or crevice on the tertiary structure where the substrate binds to form an Enzyme-Substrate (ES) complex.

• Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity:

  - Temperature & pH: Exhibit optimum curves; extreme high temperature denatures proteins.

  - Substrate Concentration: Velocity increases up to Vmax. Km (Michaelis-Menten constant) represents substrate concentration at half Vmax.

• Inhibition: Competitive Inhibition occurs when inhibitor closely resembles the substrate (e.g., Malonate inhibiting Succinic Dehydrogenase).

• Cofactors: Non-protein constituents required for activity.

  - Prosthetic groups: Tightly bound organic factors (e.g., Haem in peroxidase/catalase).

  - Co-enzymes: Transiently bound organic factors (e.g., NAD, NADP containing Niacin).

  - Metal ions: Form coordination bonds (e.g., Zinc for Carboxypeptidase).

Intext & Conceptual Questions

Q1: What are secondary metabolites? Give two examples with their physiological or commercial utility.
Answer:
Secondary metabolites are organic compounds produced by plants, fungi, and microorganisms that are not directly involved in normal growth, development, or reproduction of the organism, but have ecological functions.

Examples:
1. Vinblastine & Curcumin (Drugs): Used in medical treatments, including anticancer therapies.
2. Rubber & Gums (Polymeric substances): Extensively used in commercial industries and manufacturing.

Textbook Chapter End Exercise Questions

Q1: Distinguish between Primary Metabolites and Secondary Metabolites.
Answer:
Feature Primary Metabolites Secondary Metabolites
Role in Organism Have identifiable functions in direct physiological processes. No direct primary function; useful ecologically or commercially.
Distribution Universal across all living cells/organisms. Restricted to specific species of plants, fungi, or microbes.
Examples Glucose, Alanine, Adenine, Lipids. Morphine, Carotenoids, Abrin, Concanavalin A, Rubber.
Q2: Differentiate between Cofactor, Coenzyme, and Prosthetic Group.
Answer:
Feature Prosthetic Group Co-enzyme Metal Ion Cofactor
Nature Organic molecule. Organic molecule (often derived from vitamins). Inorganic metal ions.
Attachment to Apoenzyme Tightly and permanently bound. Transient and loosely attached during catalysis. Forms coordination bonds at active site.
Example Haem group in Peroxidase and Catalase. NAD and NADP (containing Niacin). Zinc ion in Carboxypeptidase.
