NCERT Solutions for Class 9 History Chapter 4 Forest Society and Colonialism

NCERT Solutions for Class 9 History Chapter 4 covers Why Deforestation?, The Rise of Scientific Forestry, How were the Lives of People Affected?, Rebellion in the Forest (Bastar Rebellion), and Forest Transformations in Java.

Overview of Topics & Subtopics (Click to Jump)

  • 1. Reasons for Deforestation & Rise of Scientific Forestry
  • 2. Impact of Forest Laws on Local Communities & Bastar Rebellion
  • 3. Forest Transformations in Java under Dutch Colonial Rule

1. Reasons for Deforestation & Rise of Scientific Forestry

Short Note:

• Reasons for Deforestation: British rulers cleared forests for expansion of agriculture (revenue), timber supply for Royal Navy ships, railway sleepers, and tea/coffee plantations.

• Dietrich Brandis & Scientific Forestry: German expert Dietrich Brandis was appointed First Inspector General of Forests in India. He set up the Imperial Forest Research Institute at Dehradun (1906). Natural forests were cleared and single species trees like Teak and Sal were planted in straight lines (Scientific Forestry).

• Indian Forest Act (1878): Divided forests into 3 categories: Reserved Forests (best forests, local people not allowed), Protected Forests, and Village Forests.

2. Impact of Forest Laws on Local Communities & Bastar Rebellion

Short Note:

• Impact on Villagers: Shifting cultivation (Podu/Jhum) was banned, grazing animals and collecting fuelwood became illegal, leading to severe hardship and harassment by forest guards.

• Rebellion in Bastar (1910): People of Bastar in Chhattisgarh led by Gunda Dhur revolted against the reservation of two-thirds of the forest in 1905. Mango boughs, earth lumps, and chillies were circulated to mobilize villagers against British troops.

3. Forest Transformations in Java under Dutch Colonial Rule

Short Note:

• Blandongdiensten System: The Dutch imposed taxes on land cultivated in forests in Java (Indonesia) and exempted villages if they provided free labor for cutting and transporting teak timber.

• Saminist Movement: Surontiko Samin of Randublatung village challenged Dutch state ownership of forests, arguing that wind, water, earth, and wood were not created by the state. His followers protested by lying down on their land when Dutch surveyors arrived.

Intext & Conceptual Questions

Q1: What was Scientific Forestry? How did it affect natural forest biodiversity?
Answer:
1. Definition: Under Scientific Forestry introduced by Dietrich Brandis, natural forests with diverse tree species were cut down and replaced with a single species planted in neat rows (plantations).
2. Impact on Biodiversity: Forest officials managed these trees systematically for timber production. However, it severely destroyed forest biodiversity, ecological balance, and destroyed native animal habitats.
Q2: How were the lives of local villagers affected by the Forest Act of 1878?
Answer:
1. Loss of Access: Villagers were barred from entering Reserved Forests to collect firewood, fruits, fodder, or medicinal herbs.
2. Ban on Shifting Cultivation: Swidden or shifting cultivation was banned, forcing thousands of tribal families out of their ancestral lands.
3. Harassment: Villagers taking wood were at the mercy of forest guards and police who demanded free food and bribes.

Textbook Chapter End Exercise Questions

Q1: Discuss how the changes in forest management in the colonial period affected the following groups: (a) Shifting cultivators (b) Nomadic and pastoralist communities (c) Plantation owners (d) Kings/British officials engaged in hunting.
Answer:
• (a) Shifting Cultivators: European colonists considered shifting cultivation harmful for timber trees and fire control. It was banned, forcing many communities to leave their occupations and move to other regions.
• (b) Nomadic & Pastoralist Communities: Their grazing rights were severely restricted. Their traditional routes were blocked, and many were branded as 'Criminal Tribes' forced to work in factories and plantations.
• (c) Plantation Owners: Large tracts of forest land were given at cheap rates to European planters to grow tea, coffee, and rubber, yielding huge profits.
• (d) Kings & British Officials Engaged in Hunting: While traditional hunting by forest dwellers was prohibited, British officials and Indian royalty hunted tigers and leopards in huge numbers, viewing it as a symbol of civilizing India.
Q2: What were the similarities between colonial management of forests in Bastar and Java?
Answer:
1. Monopoly & Control: Both the British in Bastar and the Dutch in Java enacted strict forest laws to control timber resources and restrict villager access.
2. Displacement & Forced Labour: Both regimes banned shifting cultivation, displaced forest dwellers, and forced villagers to provide free labor (e.g., Blandongdiensten in Java and forced labor in Bastar).
3. Peasant Rebellions: Both faced major armed or non-violent tribal resistance—Gunda Dhur in Bastar (1910) and Surontiko Samin in Java (1890s).
Q3: Between 1880 and 1920, forest cover in the Indian subcontinent declined by 9.7 million hectares. Discuss the causes for this decline.
Answer:
1. Railway Expansion: Wooden sleepers were required to hold railway tracks together. Each mile of track required 1,760 to 2,000 sleepers, leading to massive tree felling.
2. Shipbuilding for British Navy: Royal Navy timber shortages led to widespread harvesting of Indian Oak, Teak, and Sal trees for British ships.
3. Agricultural Expansion: The British encouraged clearing forests to expand commercial farming (cotton, wheat, sugar) to increase land revenue.
4. Commercial Plantations: Vast forest zones were cleared to set up European-owned tea, coffee, and rubber plantations.
