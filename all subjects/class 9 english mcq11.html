Poem 2: Wind 30 Questions

1. Who is the poet of "Wind"?

  • A. Subramania Bharati
  • B. Robert Frost
  • C. William Wordsworth
  • D. Ted Hughes

Answer: A. Subramania Bharati

Explanation: "Wind" was written by the celebrated Tamil poet Subramania Bharati, and this version is a translation into English.

2. How is the wind depicted at the start of the poem in terms of its behaviour?

  • A. As a destructive and mocking force that breaks weak objects
  • B. As gentle and calm at all times
  • C. As invisible and unnoticeable
  • D. As entirely helpful with no negative effects

Answer: A. As a destructive and mocking force that breaks weak objects

Explanation: The wind is personified in the poem as a powerful, almost mocking force that breaks and destroys weak, fragile things in its path.

3. What kind of objects does the poem describe as being broken by the strong wind?

  • A. Weak shutters, doors, and fragile houses
  • B. Strong mountains
  • C. Metal structures
  • D. Ocean waves

Answer: A. Weak shutters, doors, and fragile houses

Explanation: The poem describes how strong winds can easily destroy poorly built or weak structures like shutters and doors, symbolising the destructive power of adversity.

4. What does the wind symbolise more broadly in the poem, beyond its literal meaning?

  • A. Life's challenges, obstacles, and difficulties that test one's strength
  • B. A gentle breeze with no symbolic meaning
  • C. A symbol of peace and tranquility only
  • D. A representation of happiness

Answer: A. Life's challenges, obstacles, and difficulties that test one's strength

Explanation: The wind serves as a metaphor for the hardships and challenges people face in life, testing their inner strength and resilience.

5. What advice does the poet give regarding how to deal with the powerful wind, symbolically representing life's challenges?

  • A. To build inner strength so that one can withstand challenges rather than being broken by them
  • B. To avoid all challenges and hide away permanently
  • C. To complain about difficulties instead of overcoming them
  • D. To rely solely on others for protection from difficulties

Answer: A. To build inner strength so that one can withstand challenges rather than being broken by them

Explanation: The poet encourages readers to develop resilience and inner strength, so they can face life's challenges, symbolised by the wind, without being defeated.

6. What does the poet suggest about weak and fragile things in relation to the wind?

  • A. They are the ones most likely to be destroyed by the wind's force
  • B. They are entirely unaffected by the wind
  • C. They become stronger because of the wind
  • D. They are protected automatically from any harm

Answer: A. They are the ones most likely to be destroyed by the wind's force

Explanation: The poem highlights that it is specifically weak and poorly constructed things that fall victim to the wind's destructive power, unlike sturdier structures.

7. What poetic device is used when the wind is described as if it were a mocking, living being?

  • A. Personification
  • B. Onomatopoeia
  • C. Simile
  • D. Hyperbole only

Answer: A. Personification

Explanation: The poem uses personification by attributing human-like qualities, such as mockery and laughter, to the wind, making it seem like a conscious force.

8. According to the poem, what should one do in order to survive and thrive despite the wind's force?

  • A. Build a strong, resilient character and firm foundation, both physically and metaphorically
  • B. Avoid the wind entirely by staying indoors forever
  • C. Fight against the wind with physical force alone
  • D. Ignore the wind and hope it goes away

Answer: A. Build a strong, resilient character and firm foundation, both physically and metaphorically

Explanation: The poem advocates building a strong, resilient character, akin to constructing a sturdy house, to withstand and overcome life's inevitable challenges.

9. What does the poet say about the wind's attitude towards weak hearts and lives, using a metaphorical comparison?

  • A. The wind mocks and finds the weak hearts and lives to be laughable in their fragility
  • B. The wind respects and protects the weak
  • C. The wind has no interest in weak or strong things
  • D. The wind only affects physical objects, not people

Answer: A. The wind mocks and finds the weak hearts and lives to be laughable in their fragility

Explanation: The poet extends the metaphor of the wind to human lives, suggesting that weak-hearted individuals, like fragile structures, are vulnerable to being overwhelmed by life's hardships.

10. What does the poet urge in relation to the flickering flames mentioned in the poem?

  • A. He urges making the flame strong and steady so it isn't extinguished by the wind
  • B. He urges putting out all flames immediately
  • C. He suggests flames have no relevance to the poem's message
  • D. He advises avoiding fire altogether

Answer: A. He urges making the flame strong and steady so it isn't extinguished by the wind

Explanation: The poet uses the image of a flame to further illustrate his point that inner strength and steadiness can prevent one from being overcome by life's challenges.

11. What overall message does the poem convey about overcoming adversity?

  • A. Cultivating strength and resilience allows one to withstand and even befriend life's hardships
  • B. Adversity should always be avoided rather than confronted
  • C. It is impossible to overcome life's challenges
  • D. Only physical strength matters in facing challenges

Answer: A. Cultivating strength and resilience allows one to withstand and even befriend life's hardships

Explanation: The poem's central message emphasises that by building resilience, one can not only survive life's challenges but potentially transform them into positive experiences.

12. What does the poet suggest happens to the wind's power if we build strong inner resilience?

  • A. The wind, symbolising adversity, can become a source of positive energy rather than destruction
  • B. The wind's power increases regardless of our resilience
  • C. The wind disappears entirely once we are resilient
  • D. The poem does not address this transformation

Answer: A. The wind, symbolising adversity, can become a source of positive energy rather than destruction

Explanation: The poem suggests that with sufficient inner strength, what once seemed destructive, like the wind, can actually be harnessed as a beneficial and empowering force.

13. What is the tone of the poem "Wind"?

  • A. Encouraging and motivational
  • B. Sorrowful and mournful
  • C. Purely descriptive with no emotional tone
  • D. Angry and resentful

Answer: A. Encouraging and motivational

Explanation: The poem carries an encouraging, motivational tone, urging readers to build inner strength to face life's challenges with confidence.

14. What imagery does the poet use to depict the destructive power of the wind on physical structures?

  • A. Descriptions of the wind crashing through doors and scattering papers and books
  • B. Descriptions of a calm, gentle breeze
  • C. Descriptions of the wind creating beautiful music
  • D. Descriptions of the wind having no physical impact at all

Answer: A. Descriptions of the wind crashing through doors and scattering papers and books

Explanation: The poem vividly depicts the wind's destructive force through images of doors being broken and papers and books being scattered about.

15. What philosophical idea about strength and character does the poem convey through its central metaphor?

  • A. True strength comes from within and can help one face external adversities successfully
  • B. External circumstances alone determine a person's fate with no role for inner strength
  • C. Strength is irrelevant when facing powerful forces like the wind
  • D. Physical strength is more important than mental resilience

Answer: A. True strength comes from within and can help one face external adversities successfully

Explanation: The poem's central philosophical message is that inner strength and resilience are key to successfully facing and overcoming life's external challenges.

16. What does the poet suggest about the relationship between the wind and someone with strong inner resolve?

  • A. The wind may even come to seek friendship with someone who has cultivated strength
  • B. The wind will always remain hostile no matter how strong a person becomes
  • C. The wind has no relationship with human character at all
  • D. The wind will destroy even the strongest individuals without exception

Answer: A. The wind may even come to seek friendship with someone who has cultivated strength

Explanation: The poem suggests that once one builds sufficient inner strength, the once-hostile wind can transform into a friendly, supportive force in one's life.

17. What broader life lesson does the poem impart to young readers?

  • A. Developing resilience and inner strength helps one overcome the inevitable challenges of life
  • B. It is best to avoid any form of struggle or hardship
  • C. External forces always determine one's success regardless of personal effort
  • D. Weakness should be embraced rather than overcome

Answer: A. Developing resilience and inner strength helps one overcome the inevitable challenges of life

Explanation: The poem's key lesson for readers is the importance of cultivating resilience and strength of character to successfully navigate life's inevitable hardships.

18. What repeated address does the poet use throughout the poem, giving it a direct and personal tone?

  • A. Direct address to the wind itself, as though speaking to it personally
  • B. Address to an unnamed friend
  • C. Address to the reader in third person only
  • D. No direct address is used anywhere in the poem

Answer: A. Direct address to the wind itself, as though speaking to it personally

Explanation: The poet directly addresses the wind throughout the poem, giving it a personal, conversational tone as though speaking to a living entity.

19. What does the destruction caused by the wind in the poem symbolise about life's unpredictable difficulties?

  • A. That challenges can appear suddenly and test our preparedness and strength
  • B. That life is always predictable and free of challenges
  • C. That difficulties never truly test a person's character
  • D. That only physical objects are affected by life's challenges, not people

Answer: A. That challenges can appear suddenly and test our preparedness and strength

Explanation: The unpredictable and forceful nature of the wind mirrors how life's difficulties can arise unexpectedly, testing an individual's resilience and readiness.

20. What does the poem imply about the relationship between weakness and vulnerability to hardship?

  • A. Weakness makes one more susceptible to being overwhelmed by life's challenges, symbolised by the wind
  • B. Weakness has no bearing on one's ability to handle hardship
  • C. Strength makes one more vulnerable to hardship
  • D. The poem claims that hardship never actually affects anyone

Answer: A. Weakness makes one more susceptible to being overwhelmed by life's challenges, symbolised by the wind

Explanation: The poem illustrates that fragility and weakness, whether in physical structures or inner character, leave one more vulnerable to being overcome by adversity.

21. How does the poet use the contrast between weak and strong objects to strengthen the poem's message?

  • A. By showing that only weak things are destroyed, while strong, well-built things withstand the wind's force
  • B. By suggesting that both weak and strong objects are destroyed equally
  • C. By avoiding any contrast between weak and strong objects
  • D. By claiming that strong objects are more likely to be destroyed

Answer: A. By showing that only weak things are destroyed, while strong, well-built things withstand the wind's force

Explanation: This contrast reinforces the poem's central message that resilience and strength, whether in structures or character, are essential to withstanding life's adversities.

22. What is the significance of the poem's simple, direct language in conveying its message?

  • A. It makes the poem's philosophical message about resilience accessible and relatable to a wide audience
  • B. It makes the poem's meaning difficult to understand
  • C. It has no bearing on the reader's understanding of the poem
  • D. It is used to obscure the poem's true meaning

Answer: A. It makes the poem's philosophical message about resilience accessible and relatable to a wide audience

Explanation: The poem's straightforward, direct language ensures that its important message about resilience is easily understood and relatable to readers of all ages.

23. What does the poet suggest one should do rather than complain about the wind's destructive force?

  • A. Focus on strengthening oneself to withstand and rise above the challenge
  • B. Simply accept defeat without any effort
  • C. Blame external circumstances entirely for one's failures
  • D. Avoid engaging with challenges altogether

Answer: A. Focus on strengthening oneself to withstand and rise above the challenge

Explanation: Rather than lamenting the wind's destructive power, the poet encourages proactive self-improvement and strength-building as the appropriate response.

24. What connection can be drawn between the poem's theme and the concept of personal growth through adversity?

  • A. Facing and overcoming challenges, like the wind, can lead to greater personal strength and growth
  • B. Adversity has no connection to personal growth in the poem
  • C. The poem suggests avoiding all forms of adversity for growth
  • D. Personal growth is portrayed as impossible in the face of adversity

Answer: A. Facing and overcoming challenges, like the wind, can lead to greater personal strength and growth

Explanation: The poem's central theme suggests that successfully confronting and overcoming challenges, symbolised by the wind, fosters significant personal growth and resilience.

25. What does the poem suggest about the wind's role beyond mere destruction, once one is sufficiently strong?

  • A. It can become a source of positive energy and even companionship
  • B. It remains solely destructive with no positive potential
  • C. It has no role beyond destruction in the poem
  • D. It disappears completely once a person becomes strong

Answer: A. It can become a source of positive energy and even companionship

Explanation: The poem suggests a transformative possibility where the wind, once merely destructive, can become a beneficial and even friendly presence for a resilient individual.

26. What life philosophy does the poem's message about resilience align with?

  • A. The idea that inner strength and preparation are essential for successfully facing life's inevitable hardships
  • B. The idea that avoiding challenges is the best approach to life
  • C. The idea that fate alone determines outcomes with no role for personal effort
  • D. The idea that weakness should be embraced as a virtue

Answer: A. The idea that inner strength and preparation are essential for successfully facing life's inevitable hardships

Explanation: The poem's philosophy centres on the empowering idea that cultivating inner strength equips individuals to face and overcome life's inevitable challenges.

27. Why might the poem be considered particularly relevant and motivational for young students?

  • A. It encourages them to build resilience and confidence to face future challenges in life
  • B. It discourages any effort towards self-improvement
  • C. It has no practical relevance to students' lives
  • D. It suggests that young people should avoid all forms of struggle

Answer: A. It encourages them to build resilience and confidence to face future challenges in life

Explanation: The poem's motivational message about developing inner strength makes it especially relevant and inspiring for young readers preparing to face life's future challenges.

28. What technique does the poet use by repeatedly mentioning objects being broken by the wind?

  • A. Repetition, to emphasise the widespread and forceful destructive power of the wind
  • B. A single mention with no repetition at all
  • C. Repetition used solely for rhyme purposes with no deeper meaning
  • D. Avoidance of any repeated imagery throughout the poem

Answer: A. Repetition, to emphasise the widespread and forceful destructive power of the wind

Explanation: The poet's repeated imagery of destruction serves to emphasise just how forceful and pervasive the wind's destructive power can be on weak objects.

29. What can be inferred about the poet's own attitude towards overcoming life's obstacles, based on the poem's tone?

  • A. He holds an optimistic and empowering view that challenges can be overcome through inner strength
  • B. He believes obstacles are always insurmountable
  • C. He is entirely pessimistic about the possibility of overcoming hardship
  • D. He suggests avoiding challenges is the only viable option

Answer: A. He holds an optimistic and empowering view that challenges can be overcome through inner strength

Explanation: The poem's overall optimistic and encouraging tone reflects the poet's belief in the power of resilience to overcome life's obstacles.

30. What is the central theme of the poem "Wind"?

  • A. The importance of building inner strength and resilience to face and overcome life's challenges
  • B. The beauty of natural weather phenomena
  • C. The dangers of living in a windy climate
  • D. The importance of avoiding all forms of struggle

Answer: A. The importance of building inner strength and resilience to face and overcome life's challenges

Explanation: The central theme of the poem revolves around the empowering message that cultivating resilience and inner strength allows one to withstand and even transform life's adversities.
