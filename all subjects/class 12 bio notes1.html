NCERT Solutions for Class 12 Biology Chapter 1 Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants

NCERT Solutions for Class 12 Biology Chapter 1 Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants covers flower structure, micro- and megasporogenesis, pollination, double fertilisation, and seed and fruit formation.

1. Flower Structure and Pre-fertilisation Events

Short Note:

• The flower is the reproductive unit of angiosperms; the androecium (stamens) is the male reproductive part and the gynoecium (pistil/carpel) is the female reproductive part.

• Each anther is bilobed with four microsporangia that develop into pollen sacs, where microsporogenesis produces haploid microspores (pollen grains) through meiosis of microspore mother cells.

• The pollen grain has a hard outer exine (made of sporopollenin, one of the most resistant organic materials known) and a soft inner intine; at shedding it may be 2-celled (vegetative + generative cell) or 3-celled.

2. The Ovule and Megasporogenesis

Short Note:

• The ovule consists of the nucellus, one or two integuments, and a micropylar opening; inside it, a megaspore mother cell undergoes meiosis to form four megaspores, of which usually only one survives.

• The functional megaspore develops into the embryo sac (female gametophyte) through three rounds of mitotic division, forming the typical 7-celled, 8-nucleate structure with an egg apparatus (1 egg cell + 2 synergids), 3 antipodal cells, and a central cell with two polar nuclei.

3. Pollination and Fertilisation

Short Note:

• Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma; it may be autogamy (self), geitonogamy (between flowers of the same plant), or xenogamy (between genetically different plants), and is aided by agents such as wind, water, insects and animals.

• After reaching the stigma, the pollen grain germinates to form a pollen tube that grows through the style, carrying the two male gametes, and enters the ovule usually through the micropyle (porogamy).

• Double fertilisation, unique to angiosperms, involves one male gamete fusing with the egg to form the diploid zygote (syngamy), and the other fusing with the two polar nuclei to form the triploid primary endosperm nucleus (triple fusion).

4. Post-fertilisation: Endosperm, Embryo, Seed and Fruit

Short Note:

• The primary endosperm nucleus develops into the endosperm, which nourishes the developing embryo; in many dicots, the endosperm is completely consumed by the embryo, and food is stored in the cotyledons instead.

• The zygote develops into the embryo, passing through globular, heart-shaped and mature stages, while the ovule as a whole develops into the seed, and the ovary develops into the fruit.

• In some species, fruits and seeds can develop without fertilisation, a phenomenon known as apomixis, which is of interest to plant breeders as it produces seeds genetically identical to the parent, useful in hybrid seed production.

NCERT Textbook Exercise Questions

Q1: Differentiate between microsporogenesis and megasporogenesis.
Answer: Microsporogenesis is the process of formation of microspores (pollen grains) from a microspore mother cell through meiosis inside the microsporangia of the anther, producing four haploid microspores. Megasporogenesis is the formation of megaspores from a megaspore mother cell through meiosis inside the nucellus of the ovule; although four megaspores are formed, typically only one survives to develop into the embryo sac.
Q2: Explain the double fertilisation process in angiosperms.
Answer: In double fertilisation, the pollen tube releases two male gametes into the embryo sac. One male gamete fuses with the egg cell to form the diploid zygote (syngamy), while the second male gamete fuses with the two polar nuclei of the central cell to form the triploid primary endosperm nucleus (triple fusion). Since two fusion events occur in the same embryo sac, this process is called double fertilisation, a defining feature of angiosperms.
Q3: What is apomixis, and why is it useful to plant breeders?
Answer: Apomixis is a form of asexual reproduction that mimics sexual reproduction, in which seeds are produced without the fusion of gametes, often through the development of a diploid egg cell without meiosis or fertilisation. It is useful to plant breeders because it allows hybrid seeds to retain their desirable hybrid characteristics generation after generation, eliminating the need to produce hybrid seeds afresh every planting season.
Q4: Why is the pollen grain wall, particularly the exine, significant?
Answer: The exine, made of sporopollenin, is significant because it is one of the most resistant organic materials known, able to withstand high temperatures, strong acids and alkalis, and enzymatic degradation. This property allows pollen grains to be well preserved as fossils and is the basis of the science of palynology, which has applications in archaeology and allergy research.
