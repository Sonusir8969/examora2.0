NCERT Solutions for Class 12 English Chapter 2 Lost Spring

NCERT Solutions for Class 12 English Flamingo Chapter 2 Lost Spring covers Anees Jung's account of childhoods lost to poverty in the stories of Saheb and Mukesh, the theme of child labour, and important questions and answers.

  • 2. Part II: I Want to Drive a Car

1. Part I: Sometimes I Find a Rupee in the Garbage

Short Note:

• This section introduces Saheb, a young ragpicker from Seemapuri, Delhi, who searches through garbage for anything valuable, including the occasional rupee, driven by poverty rather than by choice.

• The author reflects on the irony that Saheb and other children like him left their homes in Bangladesh dreaming of a better life, only to find themselves trapped in the cycle of poverty and garbage collection in the city.

• Saheb eventually gets a job at a tea stall, earning a fixed wage, but the author notices that he loses a certain freedom that came with ragpicking, despite the apparent improvement in his circumstances.

2. Part II: I Want to Drive a Car

Short Note:

• This section focuses on Mukesh, a young boy from a family of bangle makers in Firozabad, who dreams of becoming a motor mechanic and driving a car, defying the traditional expectation that he continue in his family's hereditary occupation.

• The author describes the harsh, hazardous working conditions of the bangle industry, where workers, including many children, labour in poorly lit, unventilated environments, often losing their eyesight due to prolonged exposure to glass dust and furnace heat.

• Despite the bleak circumstances trapping most families in a cycle of debt and poverty, Mukesh's simple yet defiant dream represents a spark of hope and individual aspiration.

3. Theme & Message

Short Note:

• The chapter highlights the harsh realities of child labour and poverty in India, showing how social, economic and political factors trap children in cycles of deprivation, denying them access to education and a better future.

• It also underscores how small dreams and aspirations, like Mukesh's wish to drive a car, represent resilience and hope even amid extremely challenging circumstances.

Reading With Insight (Textbook Questions)

Q1: What is Saheb looking for in the garbage dumps, and what for does garbage mean to the ragpickers?
Answer: Saheb and other ragpickers search the garbage dumps for anything of value that they can sell, and occasionally hope to find money; for these ragpickers, garbage represents their means of daily survival and livelihood, and to them, it is far more valuable than it appears to outsiders, sometimes even described by the author as 'gold' for their means of sustenance.
Q2: What forces conspire to keep the workers in Firozabad in bondage?
Answer: The workers in Firozabad, particularly the bangle makers, are kept in bondage by a combination of poverty, lack of education, exploitative money-lenders and middlemen who trap them in cycles of debt, and government apathy or corruption, which together prevent them from organising for better conditions or breaking free from their traditional, hazardous occupation.
Q3: How does Mukesh's dream of becoming a motor mechanic differ from the fate of most children in Firozabad?
Answer: Unlike most children in Firozabad who passively accept their fate of continuing the family's traditional bangle-making occupation, Mukesh dares to dream differently, wanting to break away from this cycle and become a motor mechanic instead, showing a rare spirit of individual aspiration and hope for change despite his difficult circumstances.

Extract-Based / Short Answer Questions

Q4: What does the author mean by the ironic title 'Lost Spring'?
Answer: The title 'Lost Spring' is ironic because 'spring' typically symbolises youth, growth and renewal, but the children described in the chapter, such as Saheb and Mukesh, are denied a normal, carefree childhood due to poverty and forced labour, effectively having their 'spring' or youthful years stolen from them by circumstance.
Q5: What overall picture of poverty in India does the chapter present?
Answer: The chapter presents a stark and moving picture of urban and small-town poverty in India, showing how deep-rooted social structures, economic exploitation, and lack of access to education trap entire generations of children in cycles of labour and deprivation, denying them the opportunities that could otherwise transform their lives.
